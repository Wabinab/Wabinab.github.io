# Humankind: A Hopeful History by Rutger Bregman
afterthought history

One actually thought he is a great writer. It's not just about the good storytelling he possessed, but also the way he told his story. By supplying some well-known popular stories being told, and that most people believed (even if one didn't already know about it), he first introduced one to the 'common sense' and 'common belief', then, at the turn of the sub-chapter, he introduced the 'otherwise'. And that's when one understood he did his research. That's when one _feel_ he is trustworthy (even without checking his source). To be honest, one usually had difficulty knowing whether the author is trustworthy because when one reads, one kinda blindly trust the writer about it, unless one'd read about something that contradicts earlier in one's reading history. That's just because to cross-check something takes effort, and it's of course not easy. One easily sways one belief, one stands usually in the middle, and one usually don't stand strong to the left or the right (not just politically but in anything else). And if you can't tell me both the left and the right, one will try to come up with doubts later on, though at the time of the reading, one'll assume you're trustworthy. Anyway, that's what keeps one unable to stop reading his book. Bird by bird, sentence by sentence, it makes one want to read next, want to know what's next to come. It's so interesting one not only have what the author said to learn from him, but how he writes one also wants to learn from him. 

It's where not a single sentence had been wasted in watery words (that's common in Chinese novels because they charge their readers by the no. of words reads, so it makes sense to game the system by writing contents that can be done in 200 words, and repeat that, or add in unnecessary descriptions that had no use for later, to expand such 200 words into 4000 words, just to earn money). It's where the words are addictive -- seldom do one see any non-fiction writers wrote something so addictive you want to know what happens next, hence you go next line by next line by next line, word by word, until it satisfies at the end of the chapter. In novels, that's easy to sustain, by adding watery words so things move so slow that you start speed reading, reading 10 lines at a time, skipping 10 chapters (in Chinese novel, one chapter can range from 2000-10000 words on average, and Chinese novels usually have a variety length of chapters, with short ones around 300 chapters, medium ones around 1000, while long ones more than 5000 chapters or even 10,000 chapters) just to skip all those watery words and get to the point. But it's not so addictive as that you can't put it down between chapters -- and that's important because that's how Chinese novels kill your time so easily, and by the time you realize it, you're done for, your time. Instead, reading finish one chapter, you have a lingering moment where you can muster enough power to stop but you sure do want to come back later, after you finish your work/job/school/whatsoever. Basically, non-fiction needs time to digest what the author writes, while fiction, you can read it half asleep, forget 99% of it, and no one cares (at least for most fiction, with very few exceptions). 

What makes a bad writing? The modern ones, where chicken soup is being delivered. You already know when you read it -- they repeat a paragraph again and again for the next few paragraph, as if you need so many paragraph just to get it. For one, when one started reading that when one was introduced to it, it sound nice; but a few weeks later, one got so tired about it. It literally hijacks one's patience from continue to read such stuffs that everyone shares; plus, why repeat the stuffs that everyone already knows? (OK, maybe not everyone already knows, but sure -- _one_ already knows, and so it doesn't work anymore -- and one guess it's as ineffective for the others who already knows as well, though one don't know how many of them, one suspects there are many, given how many repeats such same information again and again). In contrast, author introduced something different from the mainstream (Jared Diamond's various books, _Sapiens_ by Yuval Noah Harari, etc.), so you learn something new, something that not many knows not because it's not useful but because people misbelieve stuffs. And that pulls off. 

And you know, people usually don't ask questions when they read book. But with author, he laid out the questions for you, force you to think about it, force you to want to know more about it. Well, if you're lazy and don't want to think for yourself, read on, to satiate your curiosity! OF course, it's better if we think a bit first, pause for a short moment, and get a preliminary answer, just to see how wrong our common sense is (and how often is it really **common _non_-sense**). 

There once was a story one read about hospitality in the past? And only in recent we'd enjoyed no hospitality. That was after the agricultural revolution, though, because most of the story that passed down was from after. Wonder, how could one link it to the contents in this book? 

There's a paragraph in _A Swim in the Pond in a Lake_ that author George Saunders also think that most people are decent. It goes like this, in the "Thoughts about 'The Nose'" seciond: 

> Everywhere we go, people are (mostly) kind and earnest and seem to believe in approximately the same things we do: responsibility, truth, neighborliness. And yet every night, on the news, etc., etc. And also, every era, in the history books, etc., etc. There is no cruel thing someone hasn't done somewhere, no story of degradation and hopelessness that hasn't been lived by someone (isn't being lived by someone right now).
>
> Personally, I've never met a person who was evil in the classic Hollywood mode... Most of the evil I've seen in the world -- most of the nastiness I've been on the receiving end of (and, for that matter, the nastiness I, myself, have inflicted on others) -- was **done by people who intended good, who thought they were doing good, by reasonable people**, staying polite, making accommodations, laboring under slight misperceptions, who haven't had the inclination or taken the time to think things through, who've been sheltered from or were blind to the negative consequences of the belief system of which they were part, bowing to expedience and/or 'commonsense' notions that have come to them via their culture and that they have failed to interrogate... (I'm leaving aside here the big offenders, the monstrous egos, the grandiose-idea-possessors, the cut off from reality by too much wealth, fame, or success, the hyperarrogant, the power-hungry-from-birth, the socio- and/or psychopathic.)

One wonder how the story of 'Ata goes. One thought it'll be in his chapters, but reading till the end, it was nowhere to be found. But oh well, probably because author thought we could always go and read _Naked Island_ ourselves if we really wanted to learn more about it than he repeating it. 

One is a pessimistic person, and one need more of this kind of book at certain stages of life continually pushing one _away from pessimism_ towards _possibility_ (no, not optimism, but away from nocebo towards _possibility_). One need to see that things may be possible, and that if one never try, if one'd convinced oneself that one'd failed, one will fail. And optimism doesn't work -- in which case when one failed, one'll fall deeper into the void of pessimism. But _possibility_ means it may or may not fail, and you won't know until you try your best to make it happen. 

On the whole, the book is a 5 out of 5. Or a 10 out of 10, if you'd prefer. It's time to see the decency in people, that people are just like you and me, than to see them as NPC, as lesser mind, as someone not as good nor as complex as us. Most are just good guys and gals, capable of communication, _just like us._ Strongly encourage you read the book.
