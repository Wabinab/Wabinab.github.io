# More Than You Know: Financial Advice by Michael Mauboussin
afterthought financial investment

The difference between investment and some others like gambling is that, in a roulette, you can calculate the expected value, but in an investment, usually not. The reason is you don't know the probability exactly, but could only classify it into groups -- up a lot, up a little, down a little, and down a lot. Trying to get deeper than that is futile and erroneous. Also, you don't really know how much it can go up. Sure, there are tools like DCA and the like to calculate "expected price", but ultimately, anything can happen in the future, unlike on a roulette which had fixed possibility of events happening, that alter its course. So, one already had a conclusion made early. In the words of Mr. Fukuoka, **you probably know nothing about the market that you can do about it!** Absolutely. Nothing. Trying to make predictions is futile. 

But what can you do when you know nothing? Do nothing? Of course not. There was once a saying one heard, is it from Stoic or from Spartans, that asked you to "assume the worst outcome." You can speculate/gamble on a stock, and you put an amount on the desk. Now, assume the worst outcome -- you lose everything. 100%. Can you afford to lose that amount? If not, change to an amount you can afford to lose. The rest is in God's hand. 

Though one says you know absolutely nothing about a market, that applies to the future (forward-looking), but not to the past and the now (backward-looking). For instance, people can show you their sentiments in the past -- you read about it in some 'meter' that captures such sentiment. Then, according to Ken Fisher, what is believed by most of the people would not happen. E.g. if everyone believes that stock market would be down a little this year for an elongated amount of time (for at least half a year -- 6 months, let's say), you could expect it to not happen, because it'll have already been priced in. Of course, reality wasn't that easy. The approximate formula is actually "the no. of people that _take action_ after seeing the news * the amount that they change based on that news." The former we could make a guesstimate based on their post on social media status and other reporting agencies, but the latter we don't really know. One rich guy/gal making a large change is equivalent to an army of poor guy each making small changes. But at the same time, a lot of ants, each taking a bite on an elephant, can kill an elephant. So, we seemed to know, but we can't really know. We don't know if most of those whom take action are the rich or the poor, we don't know the amount they make changes based on the news, we don't know who they are and what they do. We know absolutely nothing. 

**Luck** plays ALL the role in this. People said that Einstein used to say that "99% of effort, 1% of luck". One don't know if Einstein really said that, but forgetting about who mentioned that, but what that guy, probably Einstein, didn't mention was that the 1% of luck was vital to overcome the barrier. If you know quantum mechanics, you'll know what it means -- an electron at $n=1$ could gain however much energy, but it'll stay in $n=1$ forever unless it reaches the threshold to transition to $n=2$. Similarly, **you could have however much effort you put in, but you'll stay poor, unless the 1% luck kicks in.** GEICO could have all the sound principles underneath, and seemed most probable to success, but if someone killed it, either by having a better alternative to its strategy, or because the government enforced a law that prevent it from executing its strategy, and vast massacre anyone whom tried to strike and appeal for it, however improbable you think that might be, you wouldn't have seen the Warren Buffett you seen today; or at least, he wouldn't take off until later, with other opportunities. It's the 1% luck that smoothen out all the impending crisis down the road and pushed it to success. The people working on what we call "emerging countries" would've agreed. If people in the developed or developing world put in 99% effort, they perhaps put in 199% of their effort, since they don't have the 40-hours work week but much longer, in tandem with those workaholics in the developed or developing work (80-hours work week, even 96-hours work week or longer). Alas, their 1% luck had been locked out by their employer such that, even if they had the luck, it would be snatched away by their employers, and their employers went on the ride the luck that's supposed to be theirs, leaving them as poor as ever, paying them less than $5 per day. They never had the chance. 

If you think about _cause and effect_, they're generally compelling. A great writer wrote a great novel because each sentence cause the next. That's cause and effect in action. But equally, a con artist can use the art of cause and effect and cheat you. Similarly, in court, to defend their side, defense attorney/lawyer can use story to influence the judges and jury. **The trick is to tell the story first before laying out the 'meh'-looking evidence.** Ultimately, we are human, and unless you're a psychopath or equivalent that're born-devoid of emotions, you _subconsciously_ pity someone even when you don't want to consciously. And if you first tell the story, it alters how you see the evidence later. For example, if you first laid out the evidence of a convict for an undefined case, you'd sentenced him/her to whatever sentence. But there was one legendary trial lawyer called _Moe Levine_ whom had an approach he termed "whole man". **You cannot injure a part of a man, the logic goes; you only injure the whole man. A life is simply never the same after a serious injury.**[1] So for one of the largest settlements in New York history, in trying to win compensation for his client, he ended his closing argument with this: 

> _As you know, about an hour ago, we broke for lunch. I saw the bailiff come and take you all as a group to have lunch in the jury room. Then I saw the defense attorney, Mr. Horowitz. He and his client decided to go to lunch together. The judge and court clerk went to lucn. So, I turned to my client, Harold, and said, "Why don't you and I go to lunch together?" We went across the street to that little restaurant and had lunch. (Significant pause.) **Ladies and gentleman, I just had lunch with my client. He has no arms. He has to eat like a dog. Thank you very much.**

Even if the person is wrong in the first place, you'd changed your mind and pity him. Of course, in this case, he's trying to get more money from the convict; but stories allow us to twist facts around, letting you insert a _false facts_ that you'd made up, and treat it as a fact although you'd dreamed it up yourself. That's the strength of stories. 

What had it to do with investing, you'd asked? Many investors try to find the cause and effect of their stories, including the legendary Ray Dalio. A few succeeded because they got it right, but most others got it wrong and leave in the dust. **How can you be sure the cause and effect you associate with is real?** Had you ever tried to _invert_ it? Perhaps the effect is the cause, and the cause the effect? Or perhaps they're just spurious, none cause the other? How can you be sure you'd figure it out right? One don't know. At least one don't have an answer for this question. All one could do is _gamble_ that one got it right, and prepare for the worst. Or, put your stake in all the cause and effect you could think of, to "spread the risk", hoping that by spreading them out, one of it will hit. You'll certainly not grow as much as a direct hit, yes, but probably you'd not lose that much in case... And the odds are favoring you if you could define all the possibilities, which is kinda impossible if it's infinitely many and infinitely complex and infinitely complicated, but at least, cover as much as possible. 

Now, to counter one's own argument, one had to support author's "circumstance-based". In essence, some things work because not everyone was looking at it. Just like before, sentiments move market -- so when everyone was looking at it, it'd already been priced in. If everyone used it, it'd already been priced in, and hence loses its predictive power. That's why Ken Fisher asks us to develop new technology always to keep up with our predictive power. OK, not 'predictive' power, but having the odds of gambling on your side. If you hope to win the speculation, you'd to have the odds on your side, and play by large numbers, based on the law of large numbers (that states that miracles are not miracles because when you have the law of large numbers, they're doomed to happen. E.g. you met a friend from your hometown at a foreign country when you didn't communicated with him/her is far more possible than you think.) When people are not looking at the thing, they're going to have strong predictive power. When everyone's looking at it or using it, they're going to have weak predictive power. 

One had never see streaks as a function of _both_ skills and luck before. Certainly, like author mentioned, one always thought it's caused by luck; but skills, even though it seemed obvious, one perhaps failed to believe in it, perhaps because it might lead to jealousy. Now, combined chapter "Are you an Expert?" with "The Hot Hand in Investing", we know that not every thing can be improve by deliberate practice -- only those that didn't have a probabilistic outcome. So in terms of managing money, unlike what author mentioned in the latter chapter, it probably depended on luck more than skills. But why did author first mention that _experts_ money manager did no better than consensus, then latter said that money manager had skills that make them fare better? In fact, there **is** a skill in money managing that separates one from other. It's some simple ones, but no one believes in it, because it makes people _feels stupid_ to believe in it. First -- patience. Warren Buffett had already mentioned it, but how many of us have his level of patience? Second, don't deviate too much from market. There's such thing called 'benchmark risk', as defined by Ken Fisher and John M. Jennings -- the larger you deviate from market, the more risky you are. And we tend to make stupid decisions, so the probability we go down is higher than the probability we go up. In fact, if we buy individual stocks, the probability we buy a loser is 80-85%, and a winner only 10-15%! How much is the probability against us choosing the right one, and you don't even know which is right until you see it in hindsight, or have the eyes of God to look into the future. So, stay around benchmark, and deviate only as much as you can bear losing money on. Then, there are a few others, one suggests you go read up on them on _The Only Three Questions That Still Counts_ by Ken Fisher and _The Uncertainty Solution_ by John M. Jennings. They offer truer insights, not to "explode your money in 3 months" or something that looked too good to be true, but to let time take your wealth soar to a new level -- the power of 'compounding'.
