# Psychological Failure
shame-on-me principles

"Fool me once, shame on you. Fool me twice, shame on me." No, actually, "Fool me once, shame on me. Fool me twice, one didn't learn from one's mistakes." One of it, is _time_. 

Covid-19 came at just the right time to accelerate technology, particularly video meet tools, when they were taking their pace casually. People before that didn't like video, because they want to meet people in real life. And indeed, meeting people in real life reduce more stress than in video, for unknown reasons. But 'loneliness level' is probably lower if you meet a particular someone in real life than via video. Though, this hypothesis requires more experimentation to disprove, or probably, fail to disprove. 

But when we need to meet someone overseas, we usually have no choice other than resort to video conferencing. Unless you have a lot of time, and unless you're very rich, travelling to the place to meet people is a luxury, especially if they're very far away. But when you try to meet people at different time zones, there are problem with getting the time right for each and every one of us. The secret to most video conferencing is planning for it in advance, someone adding the time on a calendar, and it's sent to everyone else by email. Then, from their email, if they use the calendar app, they can check when they're booked out. 

But one didn't use a calendar app because one thought one'd become time's slave if one did. In the end, one learned a lot on translating time and got superb on it, particularly with 24h time zone. It's also an essential stuff for computer programmers: if your clients live in one time zone, sure, you'd nothing to worry about, but if they lived in different time zone, or you're planning to host a server in a different time zone, or whatever reason that required different time zones, things get more messy. And the messiness comes with translating time. Particularly, psychological failure in translating time. 

Con artists especially love people with confidence. Sure, they could trick people by showing confidence, like if you go to a doctor and the doctor showed you lots of confidence, it at least convinced my dad and mum to pursue a course, and persuaded them that the doctor knows how to deal with it, its dangerousness, and that they can felt safe in their hands. On the other hand, if a doctor don't act confidently, people will think this doctor is incompetent. But there's something call "being humble" -- and that's the importance of learning. Even though humble don't always solve the problem, it at least makes one progress. Continuing on the con artist. The above trick are the person, maybe or maybe not con artist, _emanating confidence_ to their victim. But there's another way con artist can act -- by letting the victim be so confident themselves that they trick themselves into the con. This, unfortunately, only works for educated people, only work for people with high pride, and only work for people who think they're superior to others. By educated people, one means knowledgeable. Example, one thought one knows everything about translating time, so one believe one'll get it 99% correct. Or perhaps a scientist talking to an artist, the scientist will presume (s)he's the more knowledgeable in terms of science, for who is (s)he if not a scientist? What if the artist do know some science the scientist don't? (S)He'll disregard it, most probably, because that's a shame on one's pride, and we hate hits on our pride. As for 'superior to others', it applies to everyone -- you know how it feels yourself, one don't need to explain. It worked itself because you know more about yourself but less about others, so your brain tell yourself that these NPCs aren't as complicated as you, hence you're superior to them. 

Anyway, yesterday, one planned with a friend to meet at 11 am his/her time, and _immediately on the spot_, one count, that we have 6h time difference, so \\(11 + 6 = 17 \\), which is 5 pm, but no, **my brain tend to ignore the '1' in front of the '7' and conclude it's 7, so 14h becomes 4 o'clock, 15h becomes 5 o'clock in the afternoon, and so on. Sure, One don't really think 14h is 4 o'clock, because it's nearer to the 12pm, so my brain have a safety check. And if one had planned with my friend to meet at 19h, one wouldn't translate it to 9pm either, because if we're to talk for 2 hours, it'll be 11pm, and with my parents asleep, one wouldn't want to talk and laugh at late night, so my brain will correct oneself. It's the time between 15h and 18h inclusive that's without a safety check, that one tend to _presume_ without second guessing. 

This morning, to make sure we meet at ahem ahem 7pm, one make sure one set an alarm at 6.45 pm to remind one. Then, when one do work, one kept thinking about the 7pm, and one kept inside one's head his/her 11am, but one never did the translating a second time. This morning, one read (the first half) an article about [trusting your own judgment on AI](https://www.baldurbjarnason.com/2025/trusting-your-own-judgement-on-ai/?lid=xzy5j2w2szp6) that told about how, if you interact with chatbots, you'll start to get into this kind of psychological problem, thinking what the chatbot generate is always right, and that the code you write with, say, Github Copilot, is more reliable. That you'd write faster code with an agent helping you. But the author doubt it, because we don't have a 'control' to back us up, to scientifically test if all that's true or not. Indeed, when we do self-experimentation, we only try to learn from experience, which may contain lots of biases. Reading that, one told oneself, hmm, one actually did double check all the AI data one get, and one get all sorts of AI data that one tried and didn't work until one did the plain old web search, but all this time through praising oneself for all the steps one took to make oneself be on guard of the psychological errors, one didn't ask, _did one get it wrong with translating the time?_ **Can you see how bad it is that one's mind is tricking oneself, that every time one had the chance to question oneself, one failed to, because one's mind cloud one's judgment?** That's the impending doom of psychological failure, and it applies to everyone on Earth. No, people as clever as Einstein and Elon Musk are subjected to it, and they can't get through it. They can't, and we can't, no matter how clever or how stupid one is. We're humans, after all, not machines. 

A few years ago, one had this kind of problem once, and this is the 2nd time that one can remember. That was a slightly variant type -- one met one's friend once a month, and one used to live in Europe for some time and was aware of something called "daylight saving time", which didn't exist for the decades one lived in one's time zone (near the Equator). One day, when the time really did change, one never go and search it up whether it changed or not. We used the calendar app to planned for our meeting, and it showed up in the header about the time change, but one never take a second look, because we used to meet at the same time every month, so one presumed it's the same all year round. You might think, it's shame on Daylight Saving Time -- if they hadn't had this stupid time changing mechanism, it wouldn't be a problem. But no, it's my fault, at least the majority of it is. Because one trusted it to work -- one trusted one's brain when one already knew how fallible one's brain is, that one **cannot trust one's memory, cannot trust one's judgment.** When one think back of a memory, every time we READ from it, we're actually WRITING into it at the same time, hence modifying it without us knowing. The more one READ, the more it distorts. As for judgment, being not a computer, one's brain never work perfectly. _But still one trust one's memory, trust one's judgment_, as if it's working perfectly, as if nothing gets distorted, as if it won't betray me when one's fully aware it will. That's how psychological misjudgment works -- they work **because you believe**. If you don't believe, you get anxiety, and anxiety makes you want to believe, for the sake of getting into one's comfort zone. 

The kings in researching on these psychological failures tendency, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, knew how difficult it is to go against these tendency. They happen in the subconscious, in the unconscious, and because they didn't reach our conscious, we never realize them until we use a checklist to go through it one by one, asking ourselves questions like, "When one translate the time, is one subjecting to confidence bias? (Thinking, thinking, thinking, what did one do during that time, was one confident? Did one question one's thought a second time?)" And so on and so forth down the list (which you can read on Daniel Kahneman's book, _Thinking Fast and Slow_).
