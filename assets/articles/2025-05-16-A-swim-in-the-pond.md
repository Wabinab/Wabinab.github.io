# A Swim in the Pond by the Lake by George Saunders
afterthought writing 

Before one started the book, one wanted to write some memoirs; what exactly the memoir it's about, let one keep it confidential for the moment. One'd decided to read 2 books about it. The first book one considered closely resembled what one want to write. In fact, it's a memoir in itself that one could use as an inspiration to my own memoir. How much does it help, one don't yet know; but one do know that memoir was quite interesting, as when one started reading, one couldn't stop reading it until one finishes the chapter. The book one hadn't finished, though, for if one starts, one know one can't stop. The other book one considered that could help one writing is this book. The subtitle told it: "In which 4 Russians give a master class on writing, reading, and life." So one thought, why not? 

When one started reading the book, one realized it wasn't what one wanted. In fact, it wasn't about how to write a memoir, but _fiction_. One thought, oh, this isn't going to help; but one didn't stop at that point, because at the same time one wanted to start writing one's memoir, one was also continuing the writing of fiction; in fact, translating the works of Xenoblade Chronicles from video cutscenes to writing; and that's fictitious work, that's a story. And this book might prove useful. And it did. 

Sure, things are easier said than done. To be attractive, every single line should make you like it enough to let you read the next line -- like the memoir one told earlier. It took great pain to stop reading even at the end of a chapter; and that's the only place where one could stop, that's where one sub-story ends and move on to the next sub-story -- that's the only weakness in tension. The rest of the text, they're holding together like Carbon and Fluorine atoms, the strongest bond one can get with organic molecules, that refused to react to anything else if you don't try hard enough. Sometimes, one do stop reading an attractive piece of text halfway, but that's because there's something _addictive_ in its way -- e.g. playing a video game halfway, or reading _addictive_ Chinese novels (and yes, the word is 'addictive', not 'attractive' -- check out footnotes for their differences)[1]. 

Before one finishes the books, one already started writing. One started it as a memoir, but the more one write, the more one had moral struggles -- there are things that _must_ be written down, to get them out to the public so someone can help me, someone can intercept and bring me out of one's depression cycles. Yet, one don't feel like writing them out bare, because one had such moral struggles that prevents one from writing it out so clearly. But what can one do? Hiding the facts aren't viable, because one _must_ get them out. The story won't be as interesting as the memoir one read if one omit the 'sensitive' details too. If one twisted the story around, one felt scared that one might distort one's memory as well, and by the time one finishes, one can no longer distinguish between what's real and what's fake. So one needs a persona, and one needs a character, and that character can't be me. One can attribute one's feelings to a character, and add however much fictitious accounts and however much exaggerations, but because that's not me, one can distinguish between him and me. The story needs to be as real as possible, yet it also needs to contain fake and exaggerated scenes that hide out the essentials, so you readers can't distinguish which's real and which's fake. It also needs some elements of cause and effect, so even though some are real and some are fake, they need to be _seem_ to cause one another, as if what's fake is real, and what's real is fake. In the end, that's what one feels like writing -- as a narrator, telling the story of someone from a 3rd person's perspective, as told to one by that someone (like _Joys and Sorrows: by Pablo Casals as told to Albert E. Kahn_ -- one's being Albert, listening to the stories by Pablo, but one also had some god sights that one can see some remnants of stories when Pablo wasn't yet born, or didn't yet remember much details, particularly before age 5); it'll become a **non-fictitious fiction.** One already started on that story, but there's more to do yet. And work had obstruct one from continue writing for quite a week already; but that's going to change soon, after one finishes the Xenoblade translations one'll get more time to work on that, replacing the time one usually uses to do the translating works. 

There are a few techniques that author introduced that he find might be useful for the writers and the readers. Though, mind you, it's not a 'how-to' book. You don't have to necessarily do what author says, though it's worth a try. And of the 7 stories, not every stories one had the feel for the beauty of the stories like author did. Example, _The Singers_. OMG, like his students, one feel exactly how his students feel, and even after he said it, one still can't appreciate. But one learnt a thing or three, in particular, _how do you expect him to write it out when he only knows how to write it out that way?_ That rings with how one translates Xenoblade -- one could only translate it like this due to the limitations of how one write fictitious works -- one'd never wrote fictitious before, and how do you expect one to write it? The learning process happens when one read some book that gave some advices and try it out in writing. It might or might not work, one might change it or might not. Literally, because one's practicing on the translating work, the first work that kept one's motivation high, Xenoblade 3, wasn't as good translated than latter works. Reading from the beginning to the end, you'd see how one changes one's mind on what's important on writing. Ironically, one started out with noting the details and abandoned it later; but now, one went back to noting the details when the elevation almost reaches the peak. In fact, that's the way one knows how to do. One tried another method, from _Situation and the Story_, the method of writing memoir, of going into the head of the characters, and one still did do that, but _purely_ and _only_ that per se won't work out, as one read through one's writing. It seems like there lacks action, and one don't know how to describe the action in rich adjectives than just... describe it. Say, "the sparring continued while they speak," or "his mind wandered on (insert a few sentence of what's he's thinking at this moment, putting oneself in his shoes) and, feeling a strong force exerted on his fingers, his attention focuses back only after his sword flew out of his hand and landed a few feet behind him." Not the best sentence, but about the best one could do. Though, one envy. 

One of the story in the 7 stories given was "Master and Man". It's the longest short story of the 7, and the beginning of it wasn't interesting; one just read casually, my mind wanders to think about other stuffs as one read. Then, when one reaches Chapter 5 of the 10 chapters, suddenly, one can't stop reading, one feels like there's an anchor that pulls one attention from doing something else. Time tickles past, and one'd been running late for dinner, yet one cannot stop reading it. In one go, one finishes the story until **BOOM!!!** The 'Oomph' moment came, and one was dumbstruck. One kept telling oneself, "oh shit, oh shit, how did Tolstoy did that? Oh my God, what the heck just happened?" One forgot where one is, one forgot that one feels hungry, one literally forget about time and entered 'flow' state. The story continues escalating until Chapter 9 ends; and of course, Chapter 10 was a mess, Tolstoy made a broken ending by making the peasant looked simple; but that didn't disturb how one feels from 5-9. From that point on, one thought, what makes such a lively story? Why does it pulls one in? Can one write a story like that? What's the difference between _Master and Man_ and _Harry Potter_? Is the latter popular because it also have the characteristics of the former? The former is no longer popular because it's written a century ago, and, mind you, most people read modern day's work only. Don't be blind by the availability bias that many people are reading old works -- sure they do, but as a percentage of the reading community, they're small. And of course, most people don't read (news don't count), so... Yeah, one know, social media is _much_ more addictive. 

_The Nose_ was somewhat mind-boggling too. The worse thing it does is **one almost believes it's true.** Actually, one thought the Marya from _In the Cart_ is real as well; it looked real, and seriously, one found a woman whom seems to resemble the character of the story on [Wikipedia online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariya_Oktyabrskaya). Certainly a different person, but oh, how my brain associates them together. For _The Nose_, at first, the magic things makes one knows it's not real. But at the end of the story, author says "he can't believe it as well", saying that how could this have happened like this and that. Author George says that such paragraph makes him feels that it's how the narrator rewriting a narration saying that he's not as stupid as the inner narrator that tell the story, but that's how the story's told to him, so he retold it 'in the stupid narrator's own words'. But for one, it's a different story. Such paragraph _almost_ makes one believe it's a real story that really happened in Russia; actually, a real _myth_ that was passed around. As we know, sometimes, people exaggerates. One seriously thought Gogol heard the story in real and just convey it on paper so the myth wasn't lost by the erosion of time! Seriously, caught my feet for a moment. 

_How to Read a Book_ by Mortimer Adler taught one how to read a non-fiction. This book taught one how to read a fiction. Though, one don't necessarily applies them, just like one don't necessarily applies what one learnt from Mortimer Adler. Because it's time consuming and requires tremendous effort! Of course, if one really needs to, and if one can justify it, one at least knows how to read it to hopefully 'get the most out of it'. 

There are a lot more advices given, other than (1) revise it more, and (2) get the cause and effect correct, elevating the cause and effect from sentence to the next. One'll not state all of them here; you should read the book to experience it in full. Though there's one thing to say -- one believes it'd helped one on one's writing, esp. fiction, but who says it can't be applied to non-fiction? A fictitious non-fiction, maybe? Or a non-fictitious fiction, it doesn't matter. Play it around, it probably helps, and it's interesting, very interesting, very playful. And one like to experiment. Though, one'd some problems with calming one's heart enough to do the revising, though. You'd see that this piece of writing you've been reading is the 1st draft, unedited. That's because one don't like editing. Actually, one can't justify editing one's blogs; though, if one's to write something that one wanted people to read (one's 'memoir'), one'll have to edit it. And of course, translating Xenoblade, one did a single pass editing (the first draft is to record what one sees, while the single pass is to smooth it out -- to make it feel not like reporting facts. And because on the first pass one was busy _recording_, on the second pass, one had the time to think about the cause and effect and how to make the story more a story, oblivious to the speech between the characters that's non-editable (one don't change the speech that's already readily-available in English, translated from Japanese by someone else already, and because one's translating from video to writing, not writing one's own story, one's not changing the speech, nor try to reinterpret it, unless how it sounds totally differs from what's being written in the subtitles)). 

Let's grow together.
